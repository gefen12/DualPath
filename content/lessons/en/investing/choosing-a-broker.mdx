## The two kinds of account

| | Israeli bank or broker | Broker outside Israel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Israeli tax | Usually withheld for you | Not withheld; you report it on an Israeli annual return |
| FBAR | Yes, it's a foreign account | Depends on where the account is. A US account isn't on the FBAR |
| US-listed funds | Only if they offer foreign securities to US citizens | Usually available |
| Statements | Hebrew, Israeli tax forms | English, US-style tax forms if it's a US broker |

## Questions to ask before you open an account

- **Do you accept US citizens who live in Israel?** Some brokers don't, and some US brokers close accounts when the address changes to Israel.
- **Can I buy US-listed ETFs?** Some Israeli banks limit which foreign securities US citizens can buy. Ask by name, for example an S&P 500 ETF with a US ticker.
- **What do trades and currency conversion cost?** Conversion fees between shekels and dollars often cost more than the trading fee.
- **What tax documents will I get, and in which country's format?**

<Callout type="warning">Don't open a US account using a relative's US address to look like a US resident. It can break the broker's terms and cause trouble later. Choose a broker that accepts your real address.</Callout>

## A note on keeping it simple

One account with a few holdings is easier to report in both countries than many accounts in different places. Every extra foreign account is one more line on the FBAR.
