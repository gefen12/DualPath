## Moving your own money isn't income

Transferring money from your US account to your Israeli account, or back, is not income in either country. You don't report the transfer itself as income. What you do report is any interest, dividends or gains the money earns while it's in an account.

## Where the cost really is

- **The exchange rate.** Banks and transfer services convert at their own rate, which can be noticeably worse than the market rate. The difference is a fee, even when it isn't called one.
- **Transfer fees** on both sides: the sending bank, sometimes an intermediary bank, and the receiving bank.
- **Timing.** Moving many small amounts can cost more in fixed fees than one larger transfer.

<Callout type="info">To compare, ask: "If I send $1,000 today, exactly how many shekels arrive?" That number includes every fee and the rate.</Callout>

## Source-of-funds questions

Israeli banks are required to check where larger deposits come from. Keep simple proof ready, such as a US bank statement, a sale document or a gift letter, especially for property purchases.

## Gifts and inheritances from abroad

If a **non-American** (for example a relative who isn't a US citizen) gives or leaves you more than **$100,000** in a year, you must report it to the IRS on **Form 3520**. It's not taxed, but the late-filing penalty is large: 5% of the gift per month, up to 25%. Gifts from a US citizen are the giver's reporting issue, not yours.

## Currency gains

Holding dollars and converting later can create a currency gain in US tax terms. Small personal transactions usually don't matter, but large conversions, for example from a home sale, are worth asking your CPA about.
